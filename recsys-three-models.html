The task: rank, don't classify

A feed never asks "is this pair positive?". Every time a user opens the app, it asks one question: out of ~20,000 videos, which 10 do we show right now? That is a ranking problem. Three very different "brains" can answer it:

🎧

The DJ — Popularity

Plays the biggest hits for the whole party. Doesn't know you — but hits are hits, and most people are happy most of the time.

🧮

The Analyst — Features

Looks at measurable signals: how popular, how fresh, how similar to what you watched. Learns rules that connect signals to engagement.

🫂

The Close Friend — Latent Taste

Never asks for your profile. Just watches what you (and people like you) do, and builds an intuition for your taste.

Meet the three contenders

1 · Popularity Baseline Popularity Baseline

How it thinks: count views per video in the training window, recommend the most-viewed to everyone. No learning at all.

  • Strength: brutally hard to beat on sparse data — most interactions really do go to popular items. And it works for brand-new users.
  • Weakness: everyone gets the same feed. Zero personalization, and the long tail never gets shown.
  • Role in our story: the bar. A model that can't beat the DJ doesn't deserve production.

⚙️ Under the hood: it really is just a counter

Press play — interactions stream in, counters tick, the ranking reorders itself. That's the entire model.

2 · Feature Model LightGBM

How it thinks: each (user, video) pair becomes a row of numbers — video popularity, video freshness, user activity, topic match. Gradient-boosted trees learn which combinations predict engagement, by asking thousands of tiny if/else questions on the features.

  • Strength: can use ANY signal you can compute — including content signals that solve cold-start for new videos.
  • Weakness: only as good as the features you hand-craft. Taste it can't see in a feature, it can't learn.
  • Trap: every feature must be computed from the training window only — otherwise the future leaks into training Leakage.

⚙️ Under the hood: a (user, video) pair falls through the trees

Pick a pair and watch its path:
Illustration with 2 hand-made trees. A real LightGBM grows hundreds of trees from the data, each one correcting the errors of those before it — that's the "boosting". The final score is always the same idea: a sum of small leaf values.

3 · Latent Taste Model ALS · Matrix Factorization

How it thinks: take the giant user×video table (100K × 19,738, almost all empty) and squeeze it into two small tables: every user gets a short "taste vector", every video gets one too. A match score is just the dot product between them. The vectors are learned from interactions alone — nobody defines what the dimensions mean.

  • Strength: true personalization. Finds taste patterns no hand-crafted feature describes — the classic "Netflix" recommender.
  • Weakness: a new user or new video has no interactions, so it has no vector — cold start is its blind spot.
  • Role in our story: the first model that genuinely learns personal taste, not global rules.

The Judge Machine: Hit@10

Three models, three opinions. Who's right? We can't ask the future — but we can hide a piece of the past and check who finds it.

The protocol (what we'll run on the real data)

  • 1 · Hide: for every user, hide the last video they watched (by timestamp) Leave-Last-Out. Last-by-time = a time split: train on the past, test on the future.
  • 2 · Candidates: mix the hidden video with 99 videos the user never watched 1 + 99. Ranking against all 19,738 is expensive — a sample gives the same model comparison.
  • 3 · Rank: the model orders all 100 candidates. It has no idea which one is real.
  • 4 · Score: real video in the top-10? That's a hit. Hit@10 = % of users with a hit. Why 10? That's roughly one For-You screen.
In the demo below the numbers shrink so you can see everything: ~10 candidates instead of 100, top-3 instead of top-10. Same machine, smaller wheels.

Key takeaways

  • 🏗️ Build the judge first. Models are cheap once the evaluation harness (time split + Hit@10) exists. Without it, a leaderboard is just a list of opinions.
  • 🎧 The baseline is the bar, not a ritual. In sparse implicit data, popularity is famously hard to beat. A learned model that loses to it — that's a finding, not a failure.
  • 📏 Rank, don't classify. Your 50/50 training set is a construction; the world is 0.036% dense. Accuracy on the sampled set says little — Hit@10 asks the production question.
  • ⏳ Time is the only honest split. Leave-last-out per user trains on the past and tests on the future. Every feature is computed from the training window only — or the future leaks in.
  • 🫂 ALS = taste as geometry. Users and videos become vectors in the same space; a recommendation is a dot product. Learned from behavior alone — and therefore blind to anything with no behavior yet.
  • 🔍 The leaderboard is where error analysis begins. Who did each model miss — cold users? mainstream drift? long-tail videos? That "why" is the next meeting's whole story.
